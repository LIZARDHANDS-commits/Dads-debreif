The torque gauge is the control instrument for power and indicates the aircraft’s current
power level. It is used during instrument flight in the same manner as for clearhood flight. If a
constant airspeed is maintained, the vertical speed resulting from power changes can be based 
on the approximation of 50 fpm of vertical speed for each 1% change in torque. If a constant 
altitude is maintained, changes in airspeed are based on the approximation of 2 KIAS for every 
1% change in torque.

Trimming the Harvard
Elevator --> Rudder --> Aileron
 
Proper trim technique is essential for smooth instrument flying. By 
relieving all control pressures, it is easier to hold a constant attitude, 
thus ensuring smooth and precise control. 
Trim Elevator for airspeed changes: control pressures vary with airspeed and use of 
ancillaries (speed brake, flaps and landing gear). A small torque change does not affect the trim 
appreciably, but the resulting change in airspeed requires a trim change
Trim Rudder:  constantly monitor the ball on the turn and slip and trim out any pressures 
required to keep it centered. Remember the Trim Aid Device (TAD) will not completely centre the 
ball 
Trim Technique: apply sufficient control pressure to establish the desired attitude, and then use 
the trim to relieve all control pressures, no matter how slight. Use the trim switches in a series of 
short bursts while maintaining the desired aircraft attitude
Tip: Periodically remove all pressures from the control stick and rudders to see if the a/c is 
trimmed and if you are inadvertently putting unwanted control pressures on the stick/rudder.

How to Correct Small 
Deviations in Altitude
 
E.g. Desired altitude is 5000’. What climb rate 
would you set to fix a 200’ altitude deviation?
400 fpm (slightly less than 1° pitch up)
Why fix altitude deviations so slowly? …BECAUSE IT:
- Preserves the balanced crosscheck
- Decreases possibility of overshooting the target altitude 
(prevents need for another correction)
Altitude Deviations: “Good judgment is required when you 
are deciding on the rate of correction required to reach a 
desired altitude. Do not make the corrections so rapidly that 
you overshoot the desired altitude; likewise, do not prolong a 
correction unnecessarily. As a general rule, the pitch attitude 
change should be such that the vertical speed in fpm is 
approximately double the error in ft of altitude. For example, 
if there is a 100-foot error in altitude, make a correction of 
approximately 200 fpm. As a general rule, a 1 degree 
change in pitch attitude equates to approximately 500 fpm of 
vertical speed.”[SMM 8.9 para 26]

Changing Airspeed
 
1/3 – Jan 2025
===== PDF PAGE 310 =====
Calculate
 Precise
• Well trimmed S+L – XC VSI to ensure 
no climb/descent and ball centered.
• Use current airspeed and tq as 
baseline – do not round. Eg:
• Current A/S and Tq: 204 KIAS, 
54% tq.
• Desired A/S: 180 KIAs
• Difference: 24 Knots, 12 tq
• Final: 180 Knots ~42% tq.
• If current parameters are well outside 
mid range, eg 120 or 250 KIAS, it may 
be more accurate to start with 
baseline tq (180 kias – 45%) due to 
the relationship between tq and 
airspeed (see next slide).
 
Changing Airspeed
 
Tune
  TQ and Trim
• Allow aircraft to stabilize and make a 
final adjustment to be precise. Trim 
throughout.
 
Execute
 Attitude/Altitude
• Procedure: Less than 20 Knots difference, set 
new tq. Greater than or equal to 20, Full power 
to speed up and idle/or boards to slow down.
• Anticipate the tq effects of large pwr change 
and balloon with speed break.
• As aircraft changes speed, smoothly set the 
attitude for that speed IOT maintain S+L. EG: As 
the aircraft accelerates through 180 Kias, set 
• ~  - ON - to continue to maintain altitude.
• Trim throughout

Changing Airspeed
 
A note on Airspeed/Tq Relationship
• The 2 knots per 1% Tq is a Linear 
Approximation of a Non-Linear 
Relationship – depicted in the graph to 
the right.
• This approximation is most accurate at 
mid-range speeds.
• At lower speeds a 10% increase in tq will 
result in a marked increase in KIAS, 
whereas at higher speeds a 10% increase 
in tq result in a lower change of airspeed.
• The ‘approximate tq settings’ in SMM are 
assumptions based on an ICAO standard 
day from 5-10k. As altitude increases 
and/or density altitude decreases, larger 
power settings (and higher attitudes) will 
be required for the same performance.
