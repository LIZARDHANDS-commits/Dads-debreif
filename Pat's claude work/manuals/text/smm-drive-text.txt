CHAPTER 1: STUDENT PREPARATION

1.1 INTRODUCTION

1\. Military pilots must be highly skilled and thoroughly competent. Initiative, judge- ment, trained reflexes and skillful techniques all contribute towards becoming a profes- sional military pilot.

2\. This manual is designed to help you acquire the fundamentals of basic flying in the Harvard II aircraft. There must be close coordination between the classroom and the flight line as each clarifies and enhances the other. Theory mastered in the classroom builds the knowledge and confidence required when flying the aircraft. The informa- tion given in this manual, together with the flight training device and ground school instruction, is the foundation on which your instructor will base your flying training.

3\. Conventions used in this Manual. The following conventions are used in this manual to draw your attention to specific procedures, events or practices:

NOTE

A NOTE points out a procedure, event or practice that is desirable or essential to highlight.

CAUTION

A CAUTION is meant to emphasize operating procedures or practices which, if not correctly followed, could result in damage to or destruction of equipment or legal claims against the crown.

WARNING

A WARNING is meant to emphasize operating procedures or practices which, if not correctly followed, could result in personal injury or loss of life.

1.2 PHYSICAL CONDITION AND MENTAL ATTITUDE

4\. Your physical condition is extremely important. If flying lessons are to be absorbed quickly and completely, you should have a reserve of physical stamina. Even if you are in top physical condition, the assimilation of all the information you receive in the first few days will be fatiguing. Your initial trips will not be long; try to keep your mind free to take in everything your instructor says and does.

1.3 YOUR INSTRUCTOR

5\. Your flying instructor is a highly qualified military pilot whose aim is to teach you to become a competent pilot. To this end, your instructor will expect your best effort and, when emphasis is placed on exactness, it is done for your benefit.

1.4 PREPARATION

6\. To gain the maximum benefit from your flying instruction, you must be completely prepared for the mission. Be sure that you read and understand all the reference material assigned to you. Be sure that your flying equipment is in good order and ready for use. Complete all prerequisites for the assigned lesson. Be eager and enthusiastic. Your instructor will reflect your enthusiasm.

7\. During your first week of flying training your instructor will introduce you to the most important phase of your flying career: Planning. Once you reach your first solo you will have been exposed to all the required steps to plan a safe flying mission and will be responsible to plan all subsequent missions until you graduate regardless of the platform you will fly. As a minimum you will brief the following:

a. Weather: departure/arrival aerodrome, en-route, suitable alternate regardless

of weather; b. NOTAMs: same as above; c. Aircraft Performance Data/Fuel: the take-off and landing distances will be briefed in your respective flight during the daily weather briefings. You must demonstrate awareness of those numbers despite the fact that we are flying set airspeeds. You also must know how much fuel is required to fly to your alternate and, further, display good airmanship airborne by keeping track of your fuel burn; d. Training Rules: as applicable for navigation and formation missions; e. Mission plan: you will outline the flying exercises you expect to fly for that particular mission as per the training plan and according to the actual weather/NOTAMs and other applicable restrictions. Your instructor will amend the plan as required;

f. Walk Time: as you gain experience in briefing, you will become responsible for walking to your airplane on-time. This will enhance your time assessment skills and enhance your effectiveness as a military pilot. In order to achieve this, you need to establish how long the following tasks take for every brief: (1) Briefing items a – e (10 minutes if well prepared); (2) Upgrade/Review items: by reviewing your previous missions and tak- ing good notes on debrief points, you will be able to provide the in- structor with your own plan on How to improve/mitigate past errors (15 to 20 minutes); (3) New Work: the instructor will introduce the new work to be carried- out as previously established pre-brief and will confirm with you the required time as this will vary greatly depending on the difficulty level (20 to 45 minutes); (4) Contingencies: the instructor will brief an alternate plan/new work if there is a high probability of the intended mission being disrupted (5 minutes); (5) Table Top Emergency: the instructor will give you a specific emergency scenario where you will be required to either deliver the critical check- list response (verbatim) or follow the non-critical checklist with associ- ated considerations until secured by your instructor (5 to 10 minutes).

8\. These timings are only given as a guideline and as you gain more experience it will become apparent to you that preparedness is the ultimate tool to maximize effectiveness.

9\. Harvard Fuel Planning. Pressure refueling in the Harvard II yields a total fuel load of 1067 lbs when using JP4 as per the flight manual Table 1-1-2. For planning purposes, 1000 lbs is the value to be used immediately after the PTO check. This will include allowances for Start, Taxi, and T/O. The following fuel definitions shall be employed during Harvard II operations:

a. Bingo Fuel: the minimum fuel state at which the aircraft must RTB with IFR

reserves (if required) or to arrive overhead with 200 lbs remaining. b. Joker Fuel: a fuel state above bingo fuel that is significant to the mission.

10\. During your training, you will be required to plan for, brief, and maintain aware- ness of both Joker and Bingo during the mission.

1.5 QUESTIONS

11\. Many things may occur that will seem strange to you and contrary to your former ideas about flying. Do not be afraid to ask questions.

12\. Ensure that you seek a solution to each problem, as no one can learn too much about flying. Pilots with years of experience and thousands of hours are still asking questions and are still learning.

13\. Your instructor will brief you before each lesson. In the pre-flight briefing you will be told what you will do, why you will do it, and how you should do it. Question any point that is not clear from the briefing to avoid confusion while airborne.

14\. After each trip, your instructor will review the lesson. This is your chance to clarify any mistakes and to review what you have learned. Be sure to get a complete understanding of your mistakes and the action you must take to correct them. Again, ask questions while they are still fresh in your mind.

1.6 COCKPIT FAMILIARIZATION

15\. Before you start flying the aircraft, you will have had some training in the Har- vard II Flying Training Device (FTD). Be prepared for these lessons, as they will ease the transition to the aircraft itself considerably. Wear appropriate flying clothing when reporting for FTD lessons.

16\. Sometimes, while waiting to fly, you may be without a specific assignment. Such free time may be best utilized in becoming better acquainted with the cockpit, its con- trols and its instruments. The Harvard II FTD will be your primary tool; however, there may be occasion to utilize the aircraft for cockpit familiarization. When using the air- craft for cockpit training do not operate any switches or touch the landing gear lever and ensure the cockpit explosive devices remain safetied.

17\. Be sure to obtain permission before doing cockpit drills in any aircraft.

18\. While studying the cockpit, consult the Harvard II Operating Checklist, C-12-156- 000/MC-000, and review the prescribed procedures. As you go through the checklist, visualize the movement of the controls and the readings on the instruments. Follow the checklist and try to develop a system; this will become important when you are actually flying the aircraft. The sooner you become familiar with the checklist, the cockpit ar- rangement, and the aircraft in general, the sooner your attention can be devoted to flying the aircraft.

1.7 AIRCRAFT OPERATING INSTRUCTIONS

19\. The Aircraft Operating Instructions, referred to as the flight manual (flight manual), is a Technical Order containing all the essential information relative to one particular type of aircraft. The Harvard II flight manual, C-12-156-000/MB- 000 must be used as a reference. Review the flight manual frequently, with particular atten- tion given to Part 3.

20\. This publication is divided into five parts:

a. Part 1 describes the controls and equipment. It includes diagrams of the

various aircraft systems;

b. Part 2 describes the normal handling of the aircraft and details the necessary

checks; c. Part 3 describes the handling of various emergency situations, such as the procedures to be committed to memory, used in the event of fire, engine failure, forced landing, etc.; d. Part 4 describes airframe and engine limitations and contains other relevant

operating data; and e. Part 5 describes flight-testing and is mainly used by maintenance test pilots.

21\. The Harvard II Operating Checklist is extracted primarily from Parts 2 and 3 of the flight manual. The checklist must be carried in a readable position on every trip. During the initial stages of your training the checklist will be opened to the appropriate page. As you progress through your training your instructor may allow you to complete some of the checks from memory.

1.8 SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS

22\. The overriding factor in all aspects of flying is safety. On the ramp there is usually a great deal of activity and noise. To ensure that you do not suffer ear damage, you will be required to wear ear protectors or your flying helmet while on the ramp. Con- sequently, you must keep a good lookout for vehicles or aircraft. Always use caution when walking on the ramp and in particular in front of running aircraft. Remain clear of the danger areas, the prop area, and jet exhaust of running aircraft.

23\. Before take-off, make sure that all the necessary checks are completed. Careless pilots are not only a danger to themselves but also to the occupants of other aircraft. Remember that any item omitted on a check can easily become the most important omission of your life. Do not take your responsibility lightly. For your own safety, as well as for that of others, get into the habit of thoroughly completing your checks.

24\. Loose Articles. Loose articles can be a serious flight hazard. While manoeuvring under various G conditions, anything that is loose could reposition itself and jam the controls or seat. Ensure that all the pockets in your flying suit are zipped up.

1.9 SITUATIONAL AWARENESS

25\. Situational Awareness (SA) is the perception of the elements (self, team, systems, threats, tasks) in the environment within a volume of time and space, the comprehension of their meaning, and the projection of their status in the near future. A person with “good” SA is able to construct an accurate mental picture of reality and project into the future to anticipate results from a number of potential outcomes.

26\. Pilots should develop an accurate mental model of the aircraft in space and time. As well, a pilot should demonstrate knowledge and accurate understanding of what is

happening, take action to maintain an accurate mental model of the aircraft in space and time, recognize degraded/lost SA in self and others, and take action to recover SA. Pilots will also review root cause(s) of degraded or lost SA to prevent recurrence.

27\. Behaviours used to gain SA include: using resources to maximize information; articulating occurrences/observations relevant to mental model; seeking inputs; look- ing out the window; using displays relevant to task in a correct manner, selected in an uncluttered state and a relevant scale; and using standard call-outs.

28\. Behaviours used to maintain SA include: task prioritization; managing distrac- tions; recognizing, anticipating, and communicating deviations from normal operations; verifying assumptions; communicating situational implications to safety and accom- plishment of the task/mission; preparing for next phase of flight during lower workload periods; and rehearsing actions for situations that may compromise/degrade SA. Ref: HPMA Handbook: Module 7 – Situational Awareness

1.10 DECISION MAKING

29\. Aeronautical Decision Making (DM) encompasses the effective management of three critical resources: knowledge, attention, and time. DM in flight hinges on a con- tinuous review of the situation, and is reflected by effective responses to any changes in the situation.

30\. The DM process should be completed timely and correctly with the decision com- municated clearly and the chosen course of action implemented. The results of any decision should be evaluated and necessary modifications made based on the current situation.

31\. Awareness and implications of DM include: verbalization of strategic, operational, and tactical goals relevant to the mission; collection of facts; soliciting outside agencies for assistance; referring to available relevant documentation; taking action to maximize time available to manage situations; communicating time constraints; identifying and weighing alternatives; verbally considering impact on mission and how decisions align with goals at all levels; and challenging assumptions.

32\. The process of planning and taking action in DM includes: conceiving and com- municating a decision or plan, prioritizing tasks, and implementing the plan.

33\. Follow up actions of DM include: evaluating the results of implementation and updating/modifying the plan as the situation changes. Ref: HPMA Handbook: Module 2 – Decision Making

1.11 LOCAL FLYING REGULATIONS

34\. Besides the general flying guidelines covered in this publication, specific regula- tions are in effect at each wing. These regulations are published in various documents including Wing Flying Orders, Standards Bulletins, the Aircrew Information File, and School Orders; they cover such subjects as flying areas, traffic rules, and patterns. These regulations are written to ensure safe, efficient flying operations and must be adhered to. You will have full access to each publication. They must be read on a regular basis, as well as thoroughly understood.

1.12 PERSONAL EQUIPMENT

35\. In addition to your flying clothing, your personal flying equipment includes a hel- met, oxygen mask, parachute harness, and personal flotation device (PFD). This equip- ment must be properly cared for. The Aircrew Life Support and Equipment (ALSE) section will ensure the proper fit of your personal equipment and show you how to carry out the daily inspection. You will be assigned a rack in the ALSE section where your personal equipment is to be stowed between trips. The ALSE section will inspect your equipment regularly.

36\. Your parachute harness should be treated as though you expect to eject each trip, even though the probability of doing so is remote. You must complete a daily inspection prior to every trip. Never leave your harness where moisture, grease, or dirt may reach the straps. If it becomes dirty, greasy, or wet return it to the ALSE section.

37\. The proper fit and care of your oxygen mask and helmet are essential. If your helmet does not fit properly it will be uncomfortable and lose its protective capability. Your oxygen mask must be inspected before each trip. If you discover any deficiency it should be corrected immediately by the ALSE section.

38\. The acronym FOD is largely used in the aviation world to describe both Foreign Object Debris and Foreign Object Damage. Not all debris necessarily leads to damage but has the potential to interfere with flying operations and cause catastrophic conse- quences. The Personal Inventory Check is one of the best methods to help prevent FOD occurrences and shall be an integral part of every flight just as reviewing the current weather conditions. It consists of the thorough review and accounting of all items that are carried aboard the aircraft. All aircrew shall be aware of the content of their pockets and any articles brought into the cockpit (i.e. gloves, pubs, and pens). Additionally, use only the pockets that can be securely closed and ensure that these pockets are indeed closed before donning any Life Support Equipment. The Personal Inventory Check is critical when operating in any aircraft type but more so with ejection seat equipped air- craft. FOD in the cockpit may restrict flight controls at a critical phase of flight or inter- fere with the ejection seat mechanism at the most inappropriate moment. FOD outside the cockpit, on the flight line, may also have serious implications if ingested by air- craft engines. Therefore, all aircrew shall conduct a pre-flight Personal Inventory Check

1.14 PRE-EXTERNAL CHECK

44\. The Pre-External Check begins as you are walking out to the aircraft. Inspect the taxi route, prop area and exhaust area to ensure that they are clear of obstructions. Investigate any obvious abnormalities.

45\. Upon arrival at the aircraft, carry out the Pre-External Check in accordance with the flight manual and your checklist. Exercise extreme caution around the ejection control handle and ensure ejection seat handle safety pins are installed before proceeding with the inspection.

46\. To prevent injury, ensure your head is clear of the canopy handle, latch hooks, and guide pins when performing the pre-external check and when entering and exiting the cockpit.

1.21 PRE-FLIGHT NAVIGATION INSTRUMENT CHECKS

55\. The Harvard II instrumentation is comprised of many systems capable of built- in tests completed automatically on start up. System faults will display themselves on the RMU, EHSI, EADI, KLN900, and/or CWS panel. This requires no input from the aircrew.

56\. Fortrainingpurposesandbuildinggoodhabitsforfollowontraininginlessequipped aircraft, you will be expected to conduct the following checks during your training:

1.22 TAXIING

58\. Taxiing is the controlled movement of the aircraft on the ground under its own power. The controls used are PCL, brake, and nose-wheel steering. At normal taxiing speeds (a fast walk) and in normal wind conditions, you will not be able to feel air pressure on the control surfaces. The ailerons, elevators and rudder will have a limited effect while the aircraft is taxiing. Your instructor will demonstrate the proper speed and techniques for taxiing.

59\. There are two methods of positioning your feet on the rudder pedals when taxiing. One is to place your heels on the cockpit floor with the balls of your feet on the rudder pedals. Slide your feet up on the pedals to use the brakes. The other method is to place your heels hard against the bottom of the pedals so that you can press your toes against the top of the pedal when you wish to brake.

CAUTION

Maintaining continuous pressure on brake pedals while taxiing or dur- ing post landing roll may result in hot brakes and/or loss of braking action. This may be indicated by spongy pedals and lower than normal pedal position angle. To avoid this condition, the proper method to use the brakes of the Harvard II while taxiing is to apply pressure on the brake pedals intermittently. Every effort should be made to avoid riding the brakes continuously as it may result in loss of braking action and aircraft control. If intermittent braking does not solve the problem, the only remaining option to bring the aircraft to a full stop is to shut down the engine.

1.23 NOSE-WHEEL STEERING

60\. The aircraft can be taxied quite efficiently with and without nose-wheel steering. To use nose-wheel steering, depress and release the steering button on the control col- umn and confirm that the NWS light is illuminated on the left side of the instrument panel. The nose-wheel will align with the position of the rudders. The nose-wheel need not be in the central position to engage the steering. The amount of rudder movement towards the direction of turn determines the turning arc of the nose-wheel.

61\. Rudder displacement is required during the turn, with minor adjustments required to maintain the desired arc. The nose-wheel steering is not overly sensitive, but you must make a conscious effort not to over-control with the rudder pedals.

62\. To disengage the nose-wheel steering, press and release the nose-wheel steering button, ensuring that the NWS light has extinguished. The following restrictions must be complied with regarding the nose-wheel steering:

a. Do not operate the nose-wheel steering when taxiing above normal taxi speeds

(maximum 20 kts); and b. Verify nose-wheel steering is off prior to take-off.

1.24 DIFFERENTIAL BRAKING

63\. The brakes work independently of each other, a feature which, when combined with the nose wheel steering, makes ground control excellent.

64\. To use brakes for directional control, hold the rudder pedals neutral and apply smooth toe pressure on the appropriate brake. Turns should be made at a moderate speed while applying intermittent brake to keep the aircraft turning in the desired arc. The use of continuous brake in a turn makes the turn sharper and reduces the aircraft’s forward momentum. To straighten out from a turn, apply intermittent brake on the outside wheel. Minimum radius turns are possible through the use of power, full rudder, and differential braking. Nose wheel steering may be used to straighten the nose wheel following a minimum radius turn by engaging it approximately 10-15 degrees prior to the desired heading, and prior to centering the rudder pedals. To stop the aircraft, apply equal pressure to each brake. You may find that you need slightly more power to taxi while using differential braking alone, but you must never use power against brake.

1.25 TAXIING TECHNIQUE

65\. Obtain ATIS, noting the runway-in-use and the wind, request taxi clearance, reset the altimeter and clock if necessary, and then complete the Pre-Taxi Check. Take a good look all around for any obstructions and ensure that your prop and prop wash will not harm personnel or equipment.

66\. Engage the nose-wheel steering, hold the brakes and release the parking brake. Release the brakes and allow the aircraft to commence roll then apply brake pressure to ensure brakes are serviceable then taxi straight ahead before turning. Reduce the PCL to idle prior to turning the aircraft. A small amount of torque may be required to establish a comfortable taxi speed, but once the aircraft is rolling, idle PCL setting should provide sufficient power for taxi. Avoid the tendency to ride the brakes during the taxi.

CAUTION

To prevent ground resonance within the propeller, stabilized operation of the propeller in the 62%–80% Np range is prohibited on the ground.

67\. Directional control of the aircraft will be accomplished by using nose-wheel steer- ing or differential braking, while using power and brake to control speed. When you are clear of the ramp and taxiing to the runway, perform the instrument check as detailed in the Manual of Instrument Flying, A-OA-148-001/AGD00. While performing aircraft checks, it is not necessary to point at the particular item. Be sure to confirm proper operation of the flight instruments in both cockpits if flying dual.

68\. You should use large-radius turns as much as possible, reserving small-radius turns for parking the aircraft or manoeuvring in congested areas. Small-radius turns may require an increase in power to keep the aircraft moving. PCL movements should be positive, but smooth. The nose-wheel turning radius is approximately 15 feet; however,

avoid pivoting on the inside tire without any forward movement as it will dramatically increase the wear on that tire.CAUTION

If brake is used to make small-radius turns, the nose-wheel steering must be disengaged. Avoid locking the inside tire to prevent damage to the tire.

1.26 SAFETY PRECAUTIONS WHILE TAXIING

69\. There are many factors that affect the direction of movement of an aircraft while it is taxiing (i.e.: wind, a small snowdrift, or a low oleo or tire). Be ready to counteract any adverse turning tendency while taxiing. Nose-wheel steering and/or differential braking can control these variations in direction.

70\. Avoid the exhaust and/or prop wash of other aircraft. Foreign Object Damage (FOD) can cause structural damage to the airframe or engine.

71\. Do not normally taxi with the aircraft wing tip beyond the hard-surfaced area.

72\. Maintain a continuous listening watch with the appropriate Air Traffic Service (ATS) frequency.

73\. If you are taxiing in a strong wind, hold the control column firmly and move the ailerons to their full deflection into wind to prevent the controls from forcibly impacting their stops.

74\. While taxiing, scan the area on each side as well as in front of the aircraft. If you have any doubt about the clearance of the wing tips, stop and get someone to guide you. Use common sense and adjust the taxiing speed to suit the prevailing conditions.

75\. The aircraft captain is always responsible for the safety of the aircraft\! There is no excuse for a taxiing accident.

1.27 OVERSPEED GOVERNOR CHECK

76\. Complete this check in a non-congested area with the brakes held. Local orders may identify specific areas for this check but park the aircraft into wind if conditions permit. Deflect the flight controls into the wind while running up the engine. Any fault that is discovered during this check will result in a ground abort.

1.28 PRE-TAKE-OFF CHECK

77\. Carry out the Pre-Take-off check as detailed in your checklist. The check can be accomplished while taxiing the aircraft. Stay alert, ensuring ample attention is given to aircraft control if conducting any checks while the aircraft is taxiing.

1.29 POST-LANDING CHECK

78\. When you have taxied well clear of the active runway, initially it is recommended that you stop the aircraft, and carry out the Post-Landing Check in accordance with your checklist. However, as you progress in training and gain more experience, the Post-Landing Check can be completed while taxiing. Get the necessary radio clearances before crossing active runways and/or proceeding back to the ramp. Stay alert and taxi with caution; avoid any tendency to relax your vigilance because you are on the ground. The trip is not complete until the aircraft is parked with the engine shut down.

1.30 SHUT-DOWN CHECK

79\. Shut down the aircraft in accordance with the flight manual and your checklist.

80\. Before you leave the aircraft, make sure that the seat pin and CFS pins are installed and that the OBOGS system is off in BOTH cockpits. When operating away from Moose Jaw, ensure that the aircraft is chocked, the controls are locked, and the parking brake is set as required.

1.31 SIGNING IN

81\. Complete the post-mission tasks by signing-in the aircraft using Maintenix and Flight Pro. Ensure that the times entered are accurate and are the same in both computer programs.

CHAPTER 2: CREW/FLIGHT COORDINATION

2.1 INTRODUCTION

1\. Human Performance in Military Aviation (HPMA) is a means to increase your operational effectiveness. Throughout your military aviation career, you will be exposed to many concepts which comprise HPMA, and you will have routine briefings on this topic. Each flying unit is required to provide training (per the RCAF Flight Operations Manual), and much of the material comes from the RCAF HPMA Manual.

2\. The HPMA model includes several non-technical skills, including Mission Prepa- ration, Briefing and Debriefing, Communication, Crew/Flight Coordination, Task and Workload Management, Hazard/Threat and Error Management, Decision Making, and Situational Awareness (SA). Many of these aspects of HPMA are addressed in other chapters. This chapter focuses on Crew/Flight Coordination. Although Harvard II stu- dents and pilots train to fly the aircraft solo, the aircraft is normally flown by two crew members. It is essential that all crew members and formation members follow the ba- sic procedures described in this chapter to operate the aircraft using safe and effective crew/flight coordination.

3\. The HPMA manual defines Crew/Flight Coordination as “aircrew tasks and effort supporting a common goal while building effective working relationships, managing conflict, and providing advice and feedback.”

2.2 CREW ROLES

4\. In any mission, each crew member has a responsibility within the flight, with the objective of keeping the mission safe. For example, a clearhood mission will have one student and one instructor, for which the instructor holds the Aircraft Captain designa- tion.

5\. Each aircraft will always have a designated Aircraft Captain (AC). The AC is re- sponsible for the safe operation of the aircraft, the safety of all crew members, and completion of the assigned mission. The AC receives their qualification following a successful Annual Proficiency Check (APC) on the Harvard II, and as such, there may be two qualified ACs on board. The AC may also be a solo student. In the Harvard II, because of its dual controls, the designated AC may sit in the front or rear cockpit.

6\. In a formation consisting of multiple aircraft, there will be one formation com- mander who is responsible for the safety of the entire formation and the execution of the mission. Each aircraft within the formation will have one AC. There is further subdivi- sion in lead responsibilities (“tactical lead” vs “formation lead”) which will be further detailed in the Formation section of the SMM. The formation lead designation will al- ways be confirmed during the formation briefing.

7\. During student or course member (CM) training missions, there exists the instruc- tor pilot (IP) and student pilot (SP) designations. The IP will always be the AC. The SP or CM still has an important role in promoting the safety of the mission — by be- ing well prepared for the mission, disclosing their status by means of the MALA, by contributing to lookout, by maintaining situational awareness, and through communica- tion. Although the SP or CM does not carry the same responsibilities as the AC, they contribute to overall mission safety by increasing the SA of the crew. Effective crew communication is essential. When an unsafe or abnormal situation is observed, crew members MUST speak up. There must not be any ambiguity about crew roles in a sortie or formation. Prior to stepping for the mission, ensure you are aware who the AC or formation commander is.

2.3 PILOT MONITORING AND PILOT FLYING

8\. Regardless of Aircraft Captain, instructor, or student, there exists a further des- ignation, which may change throughout the mission. The pilot flying (PF) is the crew member in control of the aircraft at the time. The pilot monitoring (PM) is not currently flying, but is ALWAYS following-through with the manoeuver(s), providing a means of increasing safety.

9\. At all times while in the PM role, you are required to maintain an awareness of what is going on. At a minimum this will mean by listening and looking out to help anticipate an unsafe situation. With experience you will be able to follow through with the manoeuvre flown in addition to lookout and listening out.

10\. Outside of an instructional sortie, two pilots should work together to operate the aircraft (e.g. in busy IFR airspace). In this case, division of tasks must be made clear by the aircraft commander.

11\. Transfer of Control. An important flight safety requirement during your flying training is a clear and positive understanding of who has control of the aircraft. This is especially important in a tandem cockpit. The procedure for handing over control to the other crew member is:

a. Aircraft Commander: “YOU HAVE CONTROL.” b. Crew Member: “I HAVE CONTROL.”

12\. When the aircraft commander wishes you to relinquish control, the order is given over the intercom:

a. Aircraft Commander: “I HAVE CONTROL.” b. Crew Member: “YOU HAVE CONTROL.”

13\. Additionally, the pilot taking control shall shake the control column lightly. While in close formation this is a small but unmistakable movement of the control column. The pilot relinquishing control may then raise their hands to show the other pilot that they have indeed given up control. Control must never be relinquished until the order and the response has been given. Never be in doubt as to who is doing the flying. It should be noted that for both of these procedures the instructor normally initiates the transfer.

NOTE

Transfer of control, unless in the interest of flight safety, should not occur until the completion of the PTO check.

14\. If the instructor wants control: they shall shake the control column lightly in a rapid circular motion. The student releases control and raises their arms for the instructor to see.

15\. If the instructor wants the student to have control: they shall shake the control column lightly in a rapid circular motion. The student takes control and acknowledges receipt of controls by also shaking the control column lightly in a rapid circular motion.

20\. Communications from external agencies can occur at any time during flight. You will learn to anticipate times where communications pertinent to your aircraft are likely to occur; cockpit communications should be minimized at these times.

21\. The pilot flying should anticipate that the pilot monitoring will not adjust any in- struments or equipment without prior coordination or permission. The AC can delegate control of certain systems to coordinate assistance with various tasks. For example, the AC may use simple, directive phrases such as when adjusting radios: “your radios” or “my radios”. When checking brakes: “your brakes” or “my brakes”.

2.5 CHECKS

22\. Checks are designed to assist the pilot in ensuring all relevant items are confirmed prior to initiating manoeuvres. It is imperative that they are followed correctly and no steps are omitted. To maintain accurate aircraft control and an efficient lookout, checks will often need to be broken down into one or two items at a time.

23\. The checklist can, and in some cases shall, be referenced. The white and yellow pages are not required to be memorized, however gaining an understanding of their associated system will help you determine which checklist is appropriate. It is expected that as you gain experience white page items will be completed from memory. This will greatly increase your ability to complete manoeuvres and missions in a timely manner. When in doubt, reference the checklist to ensure items are not missed. When handling a non-critical emergency, the appropriate yellow page shall be referenced at some point to ensure all items have been covered. The red pages, which cover critical emergencies, include critical items which shall be memorized IAW the flight manual.

24\. It is important to ensure all crew members are aware of your progress when doing checks. Go through checklist items at a speed which can be followed by the least expe- rienced crew member. Completing checks slowly and accurately is more important than rushing through and missing items.

2.6 CRITICAL PHASES OF FLIGHT

26\. As defined in the RCAF FOM, the critical stages of flight include Taxi, Take-off, Departure, Level-Off, Approach, Landing, and Emergencies. These phases of flight are identified to ensure aircrew minimize distractions and focus their attention on actions that directly impact safety of flight. On the Harvard II, the critical stages of flight will include all of the aforementioned phases, and will include:

a. Inside a ¾ mile final when preparing for an approach or landing; b. During the selection of ancillaries; and c. The Go-Around until the Post-Takeoff Check is complete.

27\. In accordance with the RCAF FOM, a sterile cockpit restricts non-critical aircrew communications and activities that could distract or interfere with a crew member in the proper conduct of flight duties during a critical phase of flight. When observing sterile cockpit procedures, crew communications shall be limited to standard callouts or short focused communications that are either operational, emergency, or safety-of- flight based. The requirement for sterile cockpit is not intended to stifle necessary crew communications, but to focus crew attention during critical phases of flight. Sterile cockpit procedures shall be adhered to in all critical phases of flight.

2.7 LOOKOUT

28\. You must keep a good lookout for other aircraft. Conceivably, another aircraft could be approaching head-on at a closing speed of over 500 KIAS, or flying on a collision course at close to 250 knots. The Harvard II offers excellent visibility from the front cockpit with the only known blind spot being below the aircraft and behind the canopy bow. You must stay alert to prevent the development of any situation that could require desperate evasive action. Good lookout techniques learned early in your training will be extremely helpful throughout your career as a pilot. While maintaining a vigilant lookout, be particularly conscious of other aircraft known to be in the area. Use extra caution in areas of heavy traffic. High traffic areas include but are by no means limited to: the traffic pattern, all rejoin lines for the traffic pattern as well as instrument approaches, the radar square, local departure track, large holes on a cloudy day, or near aerodromes on a low-level navigation route. Remember that there is a blind spot beneath you. Never assume that others see you: the contributing factor in most mid-air collisions is poor lookout.

29\. Crew Concept. Each crew member is responsible for lookout. Your lookout strat- egy will differ depending on the phase you are flying (clearhood, navigation, formation, IFR), front seat or back seat, traffic pattern or practice area. Traffic awareness is rarely based solely on lookout alone. Lookout is used in conjunction with listen-out, systems which help focus your area of lookout (i.e. TAS or your EFB), and anticipation of high- threat areas. Should one crew member require a heavy focus inside the cockpit (e.g. while manipulating the KLN-900), the other must maintain a vigilant lookout.

30\. Aircraft Maneouvring. Manoeuvre the aircraft in such a way that it exposes blind spots in the vicinity of your direction of travel. For example, when initiating a clearhood descent, belly checks (initiating a bank angle greater than 90-degrees) will clear the area below the aircraft. Prior to entering a spin, a 360-degree steep turn will clear the area below. If the manoeuvre to be conducted is highly dynamic, clearing turns shall be carried out prior to commencing it.

31\. Scan Techniques. Effective lookout remains paramount for collision avoidance in the visual environment. While the development of an effective lookout technique is subjective and personalized, certain fundamental principles can guide you in establish- ing a robust approach. First, segment your lookout area into manageable sections. This could mean dividing it into hemispheres (left and right) or thirds (front, left, and right). When scanning each sector, employ a systematic technique: methodically move your gaze across the entire area, pausing periodically to allow detection of both moving and stationary threats (refer to Figure 2.1 for an example scan). After clearing a sector, re- member to continue with the rest of your clearhood crosscheck. Understanding “empty field myopia” is equally important, this is the tendency for the eye to focus at 6-10ft in the absence of specific objects. To optimize your lookout scan’s effectiveness, actively push your focus to distant objects (the horizon, clouds, a ground feature).

32\. Upon acquiring traffic in your lookout, it is important to determine if the traffic can be considered a threat. One way to do this is by determining if you have Line of Sight. Line of Sight is a measure of how fast a target moves across your windscreen. If the traffic moves quickly across your windscreen, it is considered to have high line of sight, and is less of a threat. If traffic is moving slowly across your windscreen, or not at all, it has low line of sight and should be considered a threat. Stay visual with the traffic until it is no longer a threat. Prioritize aircraft control and manoeuvre as required

to avoid collision. As soon as practical, communicate the sighted traffic with your crew member in the manner mentioned in Table 2.1.

33\. Prior to seeing an airborne threat outside, your instrument crosscheck may reveal a potential traffic conflict on your TAS or EFB. Manoeuvre the aircraft as required to provide safe separation and focus your lookout in the specific area. Inform your crew member of the alert or visually acquired threat as mentioned in Table 2.1.

CHAPTER 3: BASIC AIR HANDLING

3.1 THE AXES OF THE AIRCRAFT

1\. Having control of an aircraft means that you are free to manoeuvre the aircraft into any desired attitude. Unlike land-borne vehicles, an aircraft has the ability to pitch, roll and yaw. This movement is always about the lateral, longitudinal, or vertical axes, which pass through the Centre of Gravity (C of G).

Figure 3.1: The Axes of the Aircraft

3.2 THE CONTROL SURFACES

2\. The control surfaces can be used individually or together. They are positioned as far as possible from the C of G to give the best leverage.

3.3 APPLICATION OF THE CONTROLS

3\. Because the Harvard II is reasonably sensitive to control movement at most speeds, in most cases the term control pressure shall be used in lieu of control movement.

4\. Your instructor will demonstrate the effect and the use of the controls in various attitudes and airspeeds during flight. Normally, no one control input can manoeuvre the

29

aircraft correctly. To fly the aircraft accurately, control pressures must be coordinated and must be applied smoothly and evenly. Rough or erratic movements of any of the controls will cause the aircraft to react accordingly. When a control surface is moved out of its streamlined position, the air flowing past it causes a pressure differential on the control surface that can be felt through the control column and/or the rudder pedals.

3.4 THE CONTROL COLUMN

5\. Hold the control column lightly in a relaxed and comfortable manner. Do not grab or squeeze it. Relax your arm and hand so that you can feel any pressure that is transmitted from the control surfaces.

6\. The amount of pressure exerted on the control column by the control surfaces is governed by the speed at which the surface is traveling through the air and the degree of deflection. Conversely, the amount of pressure you have to exert on the control column varies with the pressure transmitted from the surfaces. Changes in speed and power will affect the pressures on the flight controls. From a stabilized condition of flight, an increase in power will generally have a threefold effect. The aircraft will normally want to yaw to the left, the nose will want to pitch up and the aircraft will want to slowly roll to the left. A decrease in power from stabilized flight will have the opposite effect. The slower the airspeed at the power change, the more pronounced these tendencies are. Sometimes, especially during aerobatics (higher G loadings and rapidly changing speeds), you may have to grasp the control column more firmly, but more often, all necessary pressures can be applied with your hand relaxed on the control column.

3.5 THE RUDDER PEDALS

7\. Apply pressure on the rudder pedals smoothly and progressively. Do not apply pressure on a rudder pedal and hold against it with the other foot. Relax\! When con- sidering the effects of power and speed discussed above, there will be times when the resultant tendencies of the aircraft will require only a lesser amount of pressure on one rudder instead of an application of pressure on the other rudder. During these occasions it is extremely important to think in terms of “control pressure” instead of “control movement”.

3.6 THE TRIM SYSTEM

8\. The aircraft is equipped with elevator, aileron, and rudder trim. The electric trim switch on the control column operates elevator and aileron trim. The rudder trim switch is located on the PCL. You need not memorize the direction of trim tab movement for any particular condition of flight; merely trimming off the pressure so that the aircraft flies “hands off” is sufficient.

9\. Effective Trim Techniques. Use the trim switches in a series of short bursts while maintaining the desired aircraft attitude. This prevents the possibility of over-stressing or over-controlling at high speeds. The effective use of trim becomes more and more essential as you progress to more advanced sequences. Early in your training, attempt to develop a disciplined approach to trimming the aircraft and concentrate on trimming each control surface independently of the others. The recommended order of trimming off the pressures is elevator, rudder, aileron. This allows you to be in coordinated flight while concentrating on the elimination of bank and pitch pressures. As you gain expe- rience you will learn to anticipate trim inputs in each plane for any given exercise and effective trimming will become second nature.

3.7 TRIM AID DEVICE (TAD)

10\. The rudder TAD assists directional control about the vertical axis (yaw), automati- cally trimming during airspeed and power changes. Operation of the TAD is essentially transparent to the pilot throughout the flight envelope. As the TAD operates throughout the flight, the pilot will feel the rudder pedals deflect as the TAD deflects the rudder trim tab to the desired position. Manual rudder trim is additive to the trim inputs that the TAD commands. It is important to note that the TAD will not completely trim the aircraft in yaw. On your first few trips, your instructor will show you the effectiveness of the TAD by conducting various manoeuvres with the TAD engaged and disengaged. To disengage the TAD, either move the TAD switch to the OFF position or press the trim interrupt switch. These exercises will demonstrate the effects of torque at varying speeds and power settings, reinforcing the effective use of rudder. The important thing to note is that rudder inputs, though not large, are required by the pilot to precisely control the yaw of the aircraft even with the TAD engaged.

3.8 THE SPEED BRAKE

11\. The speed brake is used for aerodynamic braking and can be extended at any air- speed or altitude. Its effectiveness is more pronounced at higher airspeeds. Pitch trim is changed through a mechanical linkage as the speed brake is extended or retracted and should counteract any large pitch change tendencies of the aircraft. There will be some buffet as the speed brake extends.

12\. The speed brake is prevented from extending with the flaps extended. If the speed brake is extended and the flaps are subsequently extended, a micro-switch is activated which subsequently retracts the speed brake. If the PCL is moved to MAX with the speed brake extended the micro-switch is also activated, retracting the speed brake.

13\. On your first few trips, your instructor will show you the comparative deceleration at various airspeeds with or without the use of speed brake. As your training progresses, you will be shown how to use the speed brake to your best advantage.

3.9 THE LANDING GEAR

14\. The Harvard II is equipped with electrically selected, hydraulically operated, tricycle- type landing gear. The selector lever is a two-position switch, acting in the normal sense: up for landing gear retraction and down for extension. Extending the landing gear in- creases drag and affects aircraft trim. The red lights on the landing gear indicators indicate when the gear or gear doors are in transit (main gear only). Additionally, a red light in the landing gear handle also indicates when the gear doors are not closed. The green lights indicate when the landing gear is down and locked.

CAUTION

You must always check your airspeed indicator before extending the landing gear to ensure that the maximum speed of 147 KIAS is not ex- ceeded.

3.10 THE FLAPS

15\. The Harvard II is equipped with electrically controlled, hydraulically operated, four segment split flaps. Two detents in the selector give 23 degrees of flap, referred to as “take-off flap (T/O)”, and 50 degrees of flap, referred to as “landing flap (LDG)”.

16\. Lowering the flaps reduces the stalling speed, changes the attitude of the aircraft, and affects the trim. The reduced stalling speed allows for a reduced lower landing speed resulting in a shorter after-landing roll. The changed nose attitude normally improves forward visibility. Because of the split flaps in the Harvard II, lowering the flaps to either the take-off or landing position produces only a slight nose-down attitude and if the attitude is not adjusted upon selection, the aircraft will balloon.

CAUTION

You must always cross-check your airspeed indicator prior to selecting flaps to ensure that the flap-limiting speed of 147 KIAS is not exceeded.

3.11 TAKE-OFF

17\. Just before obtaining take-off clearance, listen out and have a good look for other aircraft on final approach. If another aircraft is on approach, delay your request for take-off until the aircraft is past the threshold.

18\. The take-off is defined as the controlled movement of the aircraft on the runway from its starting point until it leaves the ground at flying speed. Both ground and in-flight handling techniques are involved; you must be able to make the transition smoothly.

19\. Taxi onto the runway centerline using nose-wheel steering. Allow the aircraft to roll forward slightly to ensure that the nose-wheel is straight, come to a full stop, and then disengage the nose-wheel steering. Complete the Take-Off Check as detailed in the checklist and the flight manual. Hold the brakes firmly, advance the PCL smoothly to 30% torque, ensuring that the brakes are holding.

20\. When you have completed the appropriate checks, release the brakes, smoothly advance the PCL to MAX and cross-check engine instruments while maintaining direc- tional control. Expect a slight amount of right rudder to maintain directional control. Right rudder is required during the take-off, as the TAD does not begin to operate until above 80 KIAS. Initially, you may have to make large rudder inputs to keep the aircraft straight, but as the speed increases and the controls become more effective, the rudder corrections will become smaller.

21\. Hold the control column neutral until the airspeed approaches 80 KIAS, and then apply progressive back pressure on the control column to attain the take-off attitude of approximately 5-7 degrees nose up attitude. Maintain this attitude with whatever pressure is necessary. At the take-off point, all flight controls are effective.

22\. If the correct attitude is maintained, the aircraft will lift off smoothly. Because the aircraft is accelerating rapidly, it will become airborne quite cleanly.

CAUTION

If too much back pressure is applied, the aircraft can be over-rotated beyond 7 degrees nose up. This is poor practice as the aircraft could settle back onto the runway, or the aircraft could stall immediately after leaving the ground.

23\. During the take-off roll, you should be aware of the readings on the engine in- struments and the Central Warning System (CWS) annunciator panel, but most of your attention should be concentrated outside the cockpit.

24\. As you gain experience, you may conduct rolling take-offs to expedite traffic flow. This will require completion of some portions of the Take-Off Check during the roll.

3.12 CROSSWIND TAKE-OFF

25\. A crosswind affects the aircraft during take-off. As you taxi to the take-off posi- tion, check the windsock so that you can recognize and anticipate a crosswind.

26\. The initial take-off roll is the same as for a normal take-off, except that the control column is held into wind at the start of the take-off roll. The upward aileron deflection compensates for the adverse effect of the crosswind. The control column should be held in this position until the ailerons become effective.

27\. As the airspeed increases, progressively reduce the into-wind control column pres- sure, but maintain some into-wind pressure throughout the take-off roll. The upward aileron deflection is necessary to prevent the into-wind wing (which develops more lift) from rising before the out-of-wind wing. Given the amount of dihedral of the Harvard II’s wings, it is very important to compensate for any amount of crosswind throughout the take-off roll.

28\. If the into-wind wing rises the aircraft may start to skip. A series of small bounces may develop, caused by the lateral forces produced by the excess lift on the into-wind wing. During these bounces, the crosswind causes the aircraft to skip sideways, thereby imposing severe stress on the landing gear.

Figure 3.2: The Crosswind Climb

29\. Maintain directional control with rudder, aileron and if necessary differential brak- ing. Lift the nose-wheel, and fly the aircraft off the runway at the normal speed. The lift-off should be definite to keep the aircraft from skipping.

30\. As the wheels leave the runway, the aircraft will have a tendency to drift with the wind. Keep the wings level and remove the rudder input. Begin a smooth, coordinated turn into wind to keep the aircraft tracking the runway centerline (see Figure 3.2).

3.13 INITIAL CLIMB

31\. The aircraft continues to accelerate quickly after becoming airborne. When the aircraft is safely airborne, retract the landing gear. Raise the flaps at or above 110 KIAS. When you are retracting the landing gear and flaps, the control pressures will change. Re-trim to eliminate these pressures and complete the Post Take-Off Check prior to 147 KIAS.

32\. After establishing the climb heading, maintain the take-off attitude utilizing out- side references (approximately 5-7 degrees nose-up). If the proper attitude is maintained the airspeed will increase to the normal climb speed range of 140-180 KIAS (see Figure 3.3). As the airspeed approaches the desired climb speed, adjust the attitude accordingly

(approximately 10-15 degrees nose-up depending on speed and conditions of the day). Re-trim to eliminate all control pressures.

CAUTION

When an aircraft has taken-off immediately ahead of you, anticipate the possibility of turbulence, especially with a light crosswind. Although there may be a sudden deviation in the flight attitude, do not become alarmed. Use firm control movements to correct the attitude during these deviations, and then make a shallow turn in the appropriate di- rection to fly out of the turbulence. Realign the aircraft with the original flight path. If there is a crosswind, make the turn into wind because the turbulence will have drifted downwind and away from your flight path.

33\. Do not stare at the Airspeed Indicator (EASI) when you are making pitch cor- rections. Make the pitch change you think necessary, and then hold the new attitude constant. Wait for a few moments and then check the EASI to see if the correction has had the desired effect. Because of inertia, the aircraft will not accelerate or decelerate rapidly after small pitch changes. Once you have attained the correct pitch attitude re- trim the aircraft. Hold the attitude constant using outside references and monitor the performance with occasional reference to the instruments.

34\. During the initial climb, eliminate drift if necessary. Once above the published circuit altitude, begin the first turn out of the circuit. Lookout is essential during the climb, particularly during the early stages. If the aerodrome has multiple runways, be aware of traffic using these other runways.

3.14 CLIMB TO ALTITUDE

35\. To achieve the maximum rate of climb, use an airspeed of 140 KIAS and an initial attitude of approximately 15 degrees nose up. For better forward visibility, a climb at 180 KIAS is recommended for normal clearhood flying. This equates to an initial attitude of approximately 10-12 degrees nose up (Figure 3.4).

Figure 3.4: Approximate 180 KIAS Climb Attitude

36\. Throughout the climb to altitude, the same cross-check techniques are used as for the initial climb. Because the nose attitude in the climb does leave a blind spot in front of you, you must do climbing turns to maintain a good lookout. During the turns, the loss of vertical lift becomes greater as the angle-of-bank is increased resulting in a decrease in the rate of climb. The Harvard II has excellent climb performance for a basic trainer. To maximize climb performance, use gentle climbing turns.

37\. The Climb and Level Off Check, as detailed in your checklist and flight manual, shall be completed at least once during the climb through 10,000 ft and/or after level off.

38\. The cockpit altimeter and delta P are checked to ensure that the cabin pressuriza- tion is working. As already mentioned, this can only be confirmed at altitudes above 8,000 ft MSL where a differential exists between actual and cockpit altitudes.

39\. When checking the OBOGS system, either during the climb or periodically during the flight, be sure to include the following items as part of your systems check: both the selectors on NORMAL, check the blinker, and then check the press-to-test lever. Oxygen is essential above 10,000 ft cockpit altitude; therefore, it must be checked thor- oughly and monitored often.

40\. During the climb, the altimeter should be monitored for normal operation and correct altimeter setting. On some instrument trips you will climb into the Standard Pressure Region and be required to set 29.92 on your altimeter.

41\. Due to reduced engine performance as altitude is gained, you will have to gradually decrease the pitch attitude to maintain your climb speed.

42\. Roll in and out of climbing turns smoothly and at a constant rate. Pause after each climbing turn to cross-check the attitude, airspeed and power setting, and then clear the area for the next turn. Most of your time should be spent looking out for other aircraft.

3.15 LEVEL OFF

43\. To level off at a desired altitude, use the altimeter as a guide to start the level off. At approximately 10 per cent of your indicated rate-of-climb below the desired altitude, commence the level off. For example, if the aircraft is climbing at a rate of 2000 fpm, start the level off 200 ft below the desired altitude. Lower the nose gradually to the level- flight attitude and re-trim. Reference to the horizon, with an occasional crosscheck of the flight instruments, will assist you in maintaining the proper altitude.

44\. When the airspeed is approximately 5 KIAS below the desired cruising speed, reduce power to the desired torque and re-trim.

45\. As you gain proficiency, your instructor will have you practice leveling off from climbing turns. There are three ways to do this:

a. Roll out of the turn, then level off; b. Level off, then roll out of the turn; or c. Roll out and level off simultaneously.

46\. When you are leveling off at higher altitudes, considerable time may elapse before the aircraft accelerates to the planned cruising speed. Reduce power to the required torque setting when the aircraft reaches the desired airspeed or Mach number. Avoid large pitch changes, as they will cause the airspeed to vary.

47\. Once stabilized at altitude carry out the Level Off Check and periodically during the flight, carry out the Operations Check. Remember to maintain an effective look- out during Level Off. Don’t hesitate to break down your checks as required to avoid spending a long period of time on instruments.

3.16 AIRCRAFT PERFORMANCE AND APT/PAT

48\. The primary indicators that define the performance of an aircraft in flight are head- ing, altitude and airspeed. The only two factors that directly control these performance indicators are the attitude and power; thus it can be said that attitude + power = per- formance. When considering the three primary indicators that define an aircraft’s total

performance package, heading alone can only be controlled by attitude (specifically bank). On the other hand, a direct link between attitude and power exists when con- sidering altitude and airspeed control. One may think that altitude alone is controlled by attitude (pull back on the stick and you will gain altitude, push forward on the stick and you will lose altitude). Conversely one may think that airspeed alone is controlled by power (push the PCL forward and you will go faster, pull the PCL back and you will slow down). However, when determining an aircraft’s performance (specifically altitude and airspeed), any given attitude and power setting will result in a specific rate of climb or descent at a specific airspeed. One factor does not control one variable of the aircraft’s performance in itself. For example, a change in attitude with no change in power will immediately result in a climb and a subsequent change in airspeed. On the other hand, a change in power with no change in attitude will immediately result in a change in airspeed and a subsequent change in altitude; therefore, adjustments to both attitude and power will have a combined effect on performance. In order to effectively control an aircraft’s performance, this relationship between attitude and power demands constant vigilance. If a deviation from the desired performance exists, an assessment of altitude and airspeed is necessary in determining the corrective actions required.

49\. When making changes to altitude or airspeed, determine the most logical sequence of actions to attain the desired performance. When determining the most logical se- quence of action you have a choice between Attitude, Power, then Trim (APT) or Power, Attitude, then Trim (PAT). For example, to level off from a 180 KIAS climb at 200 KIAS, you would set the Attitude for level flight and allow the aircraft to acceler- ate to 200 KIAS prior to setting the Power, then Trim the aircraft (APT). Conversely, to level off from a 180 KIAS climb at 160 KIAS you would set the Power prior to attain- ing the desired altitude, and then adjust the Attitude to level off and Trim off the control pressures (PAT). In both cases, further minor adjustments to both power and attitude may be required.

50\. Although the above examples address situations where large changes are being made, the same principals apply anytime you are maintaining or changing an altitude (or rate of descent) and airspeed. For example, if you are in the traffic pattern and you find that your airspeed is 10 KIAS fast, you might assume that a reduction in power is the only required corrective action. However, a cross-check of the altitude is also required and may prove your initial assumption wrong. An improper attitude may have resulted in a loss of altitude and subsequent speed increase. If this were the case, proper corrective action would be to raise the attitude to regain the altitude, with no associated power change. If however the altitude were correct, the required correction would be to reduce power.

51\. The concepts of APT/PAT and their relationship to an aircraft’s total performance package apply to all aspects of flying. You will be expected to employ them throughout all phases of your training. Failure to do so will normally result in chasing the aircraft performance indicators leading to excessive deviations and less than desirable aircraft performance.

3.17 STRAIGHT-AND-LEVEL FLIGHT

52\. To fly the aircraft straight-and-level, choose an attitude using outside references, and then confirm your references with a cross-check of the flight instruments. This method of controlling attitude will result in precise aircraft control.

Figure 3.5: Approximate 220 KIAS Straight and Level Flight Attitude

53\. Positioning of the windscreen frame in relation to the horizon is the most com- monly used reference. The distance between cockpit references such as the engine cowling, prop spinner, canopy bow and the horizon assists you in maintaining the cor- rect pitch attitude, while the angle between the engine cowling, canopy bow and the horizon assists you in maintaining the correct bank angle. Additionally, because you are centrally located within the aircraft, a cross-check of the wings should reveal an equal amount of horizon visible above each wing.

54\. When you are controlling the altitude for straight-and-level flight, the altimeter shows whether the pitch attitude is the correct one. If your selected attitude is incorrect, the magnitude of pitch corrections will depend on the rate and the amount of altitude deviation. A slow change indicates a small deviation requiring a small correction, and a fast change indicates a large deviation requiring a greater correction.

55\. The airspeed indicator will indicate a constant reading in steady-state level flight. An increase in airspeed normally indicates a nose-low attitude and a decrease in airspeed normally indicates a nose-high attitude.

56\. Any change in aircraft heading on the Electronic Horizontal Situation Indicator (EHSI) confirms that the wings are not in the level position and the aircraft is turning. A small angle of bank results in a slow departure from the desired heading, while a large angle of bank results in a rapid change of heading. At all times, you must be making corrections to the desired parameters for the flight profile.

57\. Do not concentrate too much on one reference. Fixing your attention in one place not only hinders your progress, but also is detrimental to flight safety because you are not maintaining a good lookout. A disciplined clearhood cross-check will ensure your time is adequately divided between lookout, attitude and internal cross-check. An example of a sound clearhood cross-check for straight and level flight would be:

a. complete a lookout scan to the front and one side of the aircraft; b. check the attitude — if it has changed, adjust it and re-trim; c. cross-check the altimeter, EHSI and ball — if necessary adjust the attitude

and re-trim; d. complete a lookout scan to the front and other side of the aircraft; e. check the attitude — if it has changed, adjust it and re-trim; f. cross-check the altimeter, EHSI and ball — if necessary adjust the attitude

and re-trim; and g. repeat the cycle.

58\. Familiarity with outside and instrument references enables you to determine the control inputs necessary to control the attitude of the aircraft. Control movements should be smooth and coordinated. Accurate control is never a static situation, but requires frequent small corrections to keep the aircraft in the desired flight path.

59\. When the aircraft is in uncoordinated flight, the ball is displaced from the centre of the Turn-and-Slip Indicator. In uncoordinated flight your body tends to lean towards one side of the cockpit, which gives you a sensation similar to that felt when rounding a corner in a car. Coordinated flight should be free of slip or skid even in a steep turn, and your body should remain in a comfortable, upright position.

60\. The clearhood cross-check is a continuous cycle of activities that will vary with the task you are performing. During lookout and cross-check, you should frequently check engine instruments, fuel and oxygen gauges, etc. as well as carry out a complete Operations Check on a regular basis as detailed in the flight manual and checklist. The thoughtful pilot has a mechanic’s appreciation of an engine and a miser’s concern for fuel. Do not compromise aircraft control and lookout when carrying out aircraft checks by focusing your attention in the cockpit for too long a period of time.

62\. For airspeed changes of less than 20 KIAS, simply adjust the PCL to the desired torque setting.

63\. Setting the Torque. When making power changes, it is desirable to develop a feel for approximate PCL positioning for desired torque settings. This will allow you to move the PCL to the approximate position while maintaining the clearhood cross- check. Once the approximate PCL position has been set, pause to allow the indications to stabilize, and then reset the PCL accurately by referring to the torque gauge.

64\. To increase the airspeed by 20 KIAS or more, advance the PCL to MAX and be prepared to counteract the torque effect of the propeller. As the airspeed increases, the pitch attitude must be reduced to maintain level flight. Adjust the PCL to the appropriate torque setting approximately 5 KIAS before the aircraft reaches the desired airspeed, and then re-trim. A minor adjustment of power may be required as a final correction.

65\. There are two methods of decreasing the airspeed by 20 KIAS or more. The first method is to set the power to the desired torque setting and extend the speed brake. Adjust the pitch attitude accordingly as the speed decreases. Retract the speed brake approximately 5 KIAS before the aircraft reaches the desired airspeed. Re-trim while allowing the airspeed to stabilize. Alternatively, reduce the PCL to idle; as the airspeed decreases, the pitch attitude must be increased to maintain level flight. At approximately 10 KIAS before the desired airspeed adjust the power to the desired torque setting and re-trim. A minor adjustment of power may be required as a final correction in either case. Remember that the TAD will not fully trim out the yaw and that minor rudder inputs may be required until fully trimmed out. The second method requires multiple power changes resulting in more rudder inputs as the power changes take effect.

You will learn to look around quickly and to establish pitch, bank, and direction simultaneously. Power changes and trim technique will become second nature. A trim change is called for when you find you have to hold continuous control column or rudder pressure, however slight.

3.19 TURNING

67\. A turn is a manoeuvre that requires the coordination of all three flight controls to change the heading of the aircraft.

68\. The three classes of turn for the Harvard II are gentle, medium, and steep:

a. Gentle: a gentle turn is one up to and including 30 degrees of bank; b. Medium: a medium turn is one over 30 degrees up to and including 45

degrees of bank; and c. Steep: a steep turn is any turn over 45 degrees of bank. It is used for rapid

changes of direction, clearing turns for aerobatics, etc.

69\. Climbing turns are normally gentle turns, while descending turns can be gentle, medium or steep. All turns are variations of the basic manoeuvre, the level turn.

70\. Entry. Before entering a turn, look around to make sure that the area is clear and that it is safe to change direction. Apply aileron and rudder pressure simultaneously in the direction of the intended turn. With this application of control pressures, the ailerons move out of the streamlined position and cause the aircraft to roll into a turn while the rudder counteracts adverse yaw.

71\. The response of the aircraft depends on the amount of pressure used on the con- trols. If you want a slow rate of roll, use light, smooth pressures. For a faster rate of roll, apply greater pressures. The pressure should be applied in proportion to the amount and rapidity of the desired change. In the early part of your training you should practice rolling into turns slowly, until you have learned to feel the pressure properly. As your proficiency increases, you may roll into the turns at a progressively faster rate.

72\. Your references selected for straight-and-level flight, throughout the speed range, are maintained during the turn. As you apply bank, keep the nose in that relative po- sition. As the bank increases, apply gentle back pressure to compensate for the loss of vertical lift. In gentle and medium turns, the required increase in back pressure is comparatively small, but in steep turns the back pressure required to hold the nose up becomes more pronounced.

73\. During the Turn. Once you have established the desired angle-of-bank, release the aileron and rudder pressure smoothly. The aircraft will remain in the turn, and the control surfaces will be in the neutral, or streamlined, position. Do not release the back pressure, because constant pressure is needed to maintain the pitch attitude. Throughout

Figure 3.6: Approximate 45 degree Bank Turn Reference

the turn, hold the bank constant and make elevator adjustments for altitude similar to those made for straight-and-level flight. During steep turns, use considerably more back pressure on the control column to maintain a constant pitch attitude. Do not re-trim during the turn.

74\. During turns you will notice that because of the tandem seating, the attitude ap- pears to be the same, regardless of the direction of the turn. Continuously perform the clearhood cross-check with this new flight attitude.

75\. The angle of attack in a turn must be increased to provide a vertical component of lift equal and opposite to the weight. As the angle of attack is increased, drag is increased and airspeed is decreased; therefore, to prevent a loss of airspeed in sustained steeper turns, sufficient power must be added to compensate for the increase in drag. The amount of power needed is directly proportional to the amount of back pressure used to maintain level flight.

3.20 DESCENDING

76\. There are an infinite number of ways to conduct a descent. Depending on the conditions and circumstances, descent profiles may be grouped into four broad classes: gliding, moderate, rapid, and enroute. Complete the Pre-Descent Check as detailed in the checklist before or shortly after beginning any of these descents. Prior to descent ensure the proper APT/PAT technique is employed.

77\. Descending Turns. As with climbing to altitude, clearing turns must be performed to keep a good lookout during a descent. For most descents, gentle turns will be adequate for safety. However when conducting a rapid descent, rolling to a much steeper angle of bank and carrying out a “belly check” will be required.

Figure 3.7: Approximate 60 degree Bank Turn Reference

78\. Gliding. An aircraft descending without power is said to be gliding. (4–6% torque simulates an engine-out glide in the Harvard II) The maximum-range still-air gliding speed for the Harvard II is approximately 125 KIAS.

79\. Leveling Off From a Glide. When the aircraft is being leveled off from a glide, the power must be increased during the level off if you wish to maintain airspeed. If you wish to level off at a higher speed, increase the power before leveling off, so that the aircraft’s momentum will help to increase the airspeed. Remember to trim.

80\. Moderate Descent. A moderate descent is one in which speed brake is not nor- mally used, and power and attitude govern the rate of descent. Such variables as distance to go, altitude to lose, fuel remaining, weather, etc. are considered in selecting power and attitude. This is the most common type of descent used. Target descent rates during a moderate descent are 2000–4000 fpm with airspeed 200–250 KIAS. Begin the descent by employing the proper APT/PAT technique. Change the attitude smoothly to prevent negative G, and re-trim.

81\. Leveling Off From a Moderate Descent. To level off from a moderate descent and maintain airspeed, increase the power steadily to the new cruise setting for that altitude while you are raising the nose to the level attitude. If the manoeuvre is well coordinated, the airspeed should remain constant. Re-trim as required during and after the level off. Employ the correct APT/PAT technique should a different airspeed be desired on level off.

82\. Rapid Descents. Rapid descents are mainly used during clearhood flying. For trips flown at medium to high altitude, a rapid descent may be used to save time. Rapid descents into warm, humid conditions can cause frosting or misting of the canopy. When these conditions are anticipated, set DEFOG to ON. The cockpit heat rheostat may be

turned to FULL HOT prior to descent if desired. Typically the use of DEFOG is for short intervals. The roll-in technique, which offers the best lookout below the aircraft, is normally used to enter the descent; the amount and rate of roll depends on how fast you wish to begin the descent. When the aircraft reaches the desired nose attitude, level the wings. Re-trim the aircraft as the speed increases and as it approaches the desired value, reduce the power to idle and extend the speed brake. Descent rates can vary, but will be around 8000 fpm at 250 KIAS and up to 15000 fpm at the limiting airspeed of the aircraft.

CAUTION

Exercise caution when conducting the rapid descent, taking into account the Harvard’s VNE.

83\. When descending through 18,000 ft, do the 18,000-foot Descending Check as de- tailed in your checklist.

84\. Leveling Off From a Rapid Descent. Recovery from a rapid descent normally requires considerable altitude and anticipation. The altitude lead in leveling off is gov- erned by the airspeed, the angle-of-dive, and the G-forces applied. Retract the speed brake and re-trim as required during and after the level off.

85\. Enroute Descent. An enroute descent is most frequently used during applied in- strument operations. It is also the suggested profile for the penetration phase of a high altitude instrument approach. It allows for a reasonably controlled, but rapid rate of descent and would be an ideal descent profile when experiencing Hypoxia or Hyperven- tilation. The descent is made at 220 KIAS and the PCL set as required to maintain 4000 fpm (approx. 4–6%).

86\. Leveling Off From an Enroute Descent. When leveling off from an enroute descent, lead the altitude by 300 to 400 ft. Normally, the airspeed required after leveling off will be lower than the speed used for descending. To level off, gradually raise the nose to the desired attitude and re-trim. Allow the airspeed to decrease to approximately 5 KIAS above the desired speed and adjust the PCL to the desired torque setting.

3.21 ATTITUDE HEADING AND REFERENCE SYSTEM (AHRS)

87\. The Attitude Heading Reference Unit (AHRU) is a digital computer that receives data from internal accelerometers and an internal fibre optic gyro it is extremely reliable, even under continued extreme manoeuvring (aerobatics, etc.). It is very rare that the magnetic compass or the attitude indicator could have precessed to the point where false indications are being presented to the pilot without any other warnings.

88\. Before commencing descent for your return to base, ensure that the heading shown under the heading pointer of the EHSI is within 10 degrees of the heading shown on the standby compass (in straight-and-level, unaccelerated flight). If the heading informa- tion is invalid, the EHSI provides a heading fault annunciation. The red letters HDG indicates invalid heading data.

89\. In the event of invalid attitude information, the EADI will provide an ATTITUDE FAIL warning. Additionally, the blue-sky raster, horizon line, sky pointer and the roll and pitch scales will be removed from the display. This display will remain for as long as the input flags indicate the data is invalid.

90\. It is particularly important to confirm the indications displayed by the magnetic compass and the attitude indicator before beginning your descent to land, especially if you anticipate encountering IMC conditions before landing. It is good airmanship to compare the readings between the magnetic and standby magnetic compass periodically to ensure that your magnetic compass is reading correctly.

CHAPTER 4: TRAFFIC PATTERNS AND LANDINGS

4.1 INTRODUCTION

1\. This chapter describes aircraft handling associated with Harvard II VFR pattern procedures, which normally include the traffic pattern, overhead break, final turn, land- ing transition, landing, and the low approach and Go Around. Additional patterns flown are the straight-in, which includes the approach phase, and closed pattern, both of which are derived from the normal patterns. All of the approaches can be flown in any flap con- figuration. To illustrate this, this manual lists final approach speeds as “100/105/110” KIAS. The numbers represent the minimum allowable airspeeds prior to round-out and are in the sequence of LDG flap, T/O flap, and flapless configurations respectively.

2\. Some airfields that you will operate from, over your career, do not permit the typ- ical military traffic patterns as flown during your training; therefore, the patterns men- tioned above can easily be modified to adapt to other established procedures. Regardless of this, published VFR pattern profiles and procedures ensure the orderly flow of the maximum number of aircraft, with the minimum congestion, around an aerodrome and runway. The traffic pattern profiles, normally published in Wing Flying Orders (WFOs) at military airfields, will include a ground track, altitude, and airspeed.

4\. Complete the Pre-Descent Check at an appropriate time during return to base. The check should be done before or shortly after beginning descent. 4.4 THE STRAIGHT-IN APPROACH

6\. Although the usual VFR procedure used in your daily flying is the overhead break, there may be times when the use of a straight-in approach is desirable. There are also many civilian airfields that may not allow the overhead break; therefore, a variation of the straight-in approach will have to be used.

4.5 JOINING FOR THE STRAIGHT-IN APPROACH

7\. The primary method of joining for the straight-in approach is via the extended base leg of the approach IAW published procedures. Entry for the approach shall be made in such a manner as to avoid “cutting off” other aircraft, conforming as closely as possible to the published ground track.

8\. When joining for the straight-in, plan your descent to be established on the ex- tended track of the base leg, on altitude and airspeed at least 1 NM from the published traffic pattern to ensure effective lookout. Traffic that is already established in the traffic pattern normally has the right of way. Base leg altitude is normally 1000 ft AGL or as published in local orders. Base leg altitude at Moose Jaw is 2700 ft MSL (approximately 800 ft AGL) in order to de-conflict with normal pattern traffic. Base leg airspeed is be- tween 120 and 147 KIAS (approximately 25% torque). If any conflict exists during the rejoin, take positive action to avoid the conflict and re-position the aircraft for another rejoin attempt.

4.6 BASE LEG

9\. Once established on the published base leg, ensure that the airspeed is below 147 KIAS. Lower the landing gear and select flaps to T/O for a normal straight-in approach. Adjust power to maintain an ideal airspeed of 120 KIAS (approximately 35% torque) and complete the pre-landing check. Trim the aircraft throughout. The 45 degree head- ing change on the entry leg is designed to make the turn to final easier to judge. Using 30-45 degrees of bank, turn to roll onto the 45 degree leg and transmit to tower (e.g. “APOLLO 4, LEFT/RIGHT BASE, STRAIGHT-IN”). See Figures 4.2 and 4.3. Com- mence a 30-45 degree bank turn in order to align the aircraft on the extended runway centreline. A small amount of power may be required during these turns in order to maintain 120 KIAS.

4.7 APPROACH

10\. Maintain base leg altitude and apply sufficient crab to track the extended runway centreline in preparation for descent. Upon intercepting a 3 degree glide path commence descent with the speed stabilized at 120 KIAS (approximately 25% torque). Trim out the control pressures. Apply the principals of PAT/APT in order to intercept and maintain the 3 degree glide path. Constantly cross-check the runway aim point and the airspeed throughout the descent (when you cross-check the aim point on approach, spend a few moments to determine if there is a trend, such as the glide path becoming steeper or shallower). If a deviation occurs, adjust the power and/or attitude as required to correct back to the ideal glide path and/or airspeed and then readjust to maintain. Pitch and power changes should be relatively small while correcting in order to minimize the possibility of chasing the parameters or inducing excessive torque changes. Continue to trim out the control pressures while making corrections. Additionally, it is important to maintain an effective lookout by scanning up and to the left/right. This will provide situational awareness of possible PFL or final turn traffic.

WARNING

Never reduce the PCL to idle until landing is assured. Idle torque will cause rapid airspeed reductions or generate excessive rates of descent and engine spool up time is quite slow. Should you suddenly need more power, it will be more readily available if the PCL is above idle.

11\. Remember to crab as required to track the extended runway centerline throughout the approach. Corrections should be made with minimal bank and assisted with rudder as required to minimize the effects of adverse yaw.

WARNING

Do not exceed 15 units of AOA while manoeuvring during any portion of the approach.

12\. As you get closer to the runway, maintain the descent while attempting to pass through an imaginary window at ¾ NM from the runway threshold on a 3 degree glide path, which is approximately 2100’–2200’ MSL in Moose Jaw. This window is a com- mon reference point regardless of the type of VFR approach flown. This window is the point at which the landing transition is initiated.

4.8 THE LANDING TRANSITION

13\. The landing transition described herein is based upon a normal straight-in ap- proach and it commences as the aircraft passes through the window. Initiate the landing transition with the selection of LDG flap and allow the airspeed to slow to the final ap- proach speed of 100 KIAS. Adjust the attitude as required to maintain the runway aim point throughout the configuration change and subsequent speed reduction. A slight power reduction may also be required to assist with the speed reduction. If landing is to be made with T/O flap, then a more pronounced power reduction will be required to get the speed to reduce to the T/O flap final approach speed of 105 KIAS. The decision to select LDG flap will be dependent upon the wind and or training requirements.

14\. The use of the Harvard II’s split flaps on final generally has a twofold effect:

a. T/O Flap. A very slight increase in lift which effectively lowers the stall speed and allows for a slower final approach speed along with an increase in drag; and b. LDG Flap. A marked increase in drag and, to a much lesser extent, a further increase in lift, allowing for an even slower final approach speed. The aircraft attitude remains relatively constant through the entire range of flap travel.

CAUTION

Large changes in pitch and significant power reductions should be avoided on short final as they can induce high rates of descent and potential hard landings. If you are significantly above the glide slope on short final, do not contin- uously steepen glideslope in order to maintain the aim point. If glides- lope control has been mismanaged, the safest action is to adjust the aim point further down the runway or go around.

4.9 THE ROUND-OUT/FLARE

15\. The actual landing begins when the airspeed is purposely reduced below final ap- proach speed. The round-out/flare is the transition from the final approach speed to the landing attitude and is normally accomplished using a combination of attitude and PCL adjustments. Cross the aim point on speed with the control surfaces trimmed. Initiate a round-out at approximately 10–15 ft above the runway by applying gentle back pres- sure to the control column to check the descent and retard the PCL to idle once landing is assured. Smoothly change the aircraft’s attitude to the landing attitude and continue gradually increasing the attitude as the airspeed decreases to prevent the aircraft from sinking. A slow continuous increase in back pressure is required to hold the aircraft just above the runway. When you have attained a slightly nose up attitude, hold this attitude constant and allow the main wheels of the aircraft to settle onto the runway. Momentary actuation of the stick shaker may occur just prior to touchdown. Airspeed will dissipate in the flare and touchdown will normally occur at 80–90 KIAS. During normal con- ditions, the touchdown should occur approximately 300–400 ft down the runway. See Figure 4.4

Figure 4.4: Final Approach and Landing

NOTE

Do not hesitate to advance the PCL to maintain final approach speed until the initiation of the flare.

4.10 THE TOUCHDOWN

16\. The touchdown should be on the main wheels with the nose-wheel off the runway. At the point of touchdown the aircraft is not fully stalled but is settling because of a gradual reduction in lift. After the main wheels are firmly on the runway, gently lower the nose-wheel before the decreasing airspeed causes a loss of elevator control. With sufficient runway available, once the airspeed has decreased below the minimum stall speed for landing configuration, the brakes may be tested by applying light toe pressure to the brakes to determine if the brakes are serviceable. Although braking may be used above 80 KIAS, great caution must be used to avoid locking the brakes. The proper braking technique involves an application of positive and steady brake pressure while easing aft on the control column and maintaining any applicable crosswind aileron input. This action will dissipate your speed and achieve a controlled deceleration to taxi speed. The Harvard II is prone to nose-wheel shimmy. If this condition is encountered, use sufficient aft pressure on the control column to minimize weight on the nose-wheel. The rudder effectiveness will decrease as airspeed decreases therefore you must transfer to the brakes for directional control before the rudder becomes ineffective. If required, nose-wheel steering should only be engaged after the aircraft has slowed to taxi speed. On completion of the after-landing roll, exit the runway in compliance with local flying orders.

WARNING

The likelihood of a blown tire resulting from brake lock is greater during increased crosswind conditions due to one wheel experiencing less force on it than the other. If sufficient runway length is available, delaying testing brakes until below the minimum stall speed for landing configu- ration is recommended. Remember, a blown tire can occur due to brake lock even at relatively low speeds.

17\. Directional Control on Landing. The following factors tend to influence direc- tional control on landing:

a. Wind. A strong crosswind causes the aircraft to turn into the wind. A gust of wind may cause a wing to rise, thus producing an ‘into wind’ turning moment.

b. Tires. If you land with a flat tire, or if a tire bursts during the after-landing roll, the braking efficiency is no longer equal and this will cause the aircraft to swing towards the side with better braking.

18\. During the landing, directional control is of vital importance and can be easily achieved. There are four controls that help you to maintain direction:

a. Rudder. The rudder serves the same purpose as in the air — it changes the aircraft’s yaw. On the ground, this yaw is directional control, thus the rudder is used to initiate, increase, or stop a turn. The effectiveness of the rudder varies directly with the speed of the aircraft, and becomes ineffective at low speed. As the speed decreases, the brakes must be used to assist in directional control. b. Ailerons. The ailerons are used to balance the lift produced by the wings. During the landing roll, moving the control column to one side reduces lift on that wing thereby increasing the downward force on the main gear on that side. This added force is effective in preventing the aircraft from turning into wind. This is a common technique used for countering a crosswind. c. Brake. It is possible to maintain direction with brakes alone. However, brakes are not normally used for directional control, unless the rudder is not sufficiently effective. The use of brakes can overcome the more violent swings, such as those from a flat tire. d. Nose-Wheel Steering. With the nose-wheel on the ground, nose-wheel steer- ing gives you positive directional control. Because of its sensitivity you should not engage the nose wheel steering until the aircraft has been slowed down to taxi speed.

CAUTION

Do not use nose-wheel steering during the after-landing roll above taxi speed, except when there is brake failure, the run- way is wet or icy, or the aircraft has a flat tire.

e. Vision. Occasionally, you will round-out too low, too high, or too quickly. Usually, these errors in judgment are a result of sight fixation. If you focus your eyes on a point that is too close to the aircraft, the tendency is to round- out high; if you look too far away, the tendency is to round-out low. You should sit erect with your head in a natural position. During the round-out, while you are bringing the aircraft into the landing attitude, vary your visual ground coverage to a moderate distance ahead of the aircraft (600–1000 ft) and take in both sides of the runway. Change your focus slowly. Your in- structor will demonstrate several approaches and landings and will point out when to start the round-out. Try to take in the whole picture and you will find, after some practice, that a certain point looks right.

4.11 LANDING IRREGULARITIES

19\. The following lists the common problems associated with landings. In all cases, recovery actions are listed, however anytime on final approach or during the landing phase, a timely decision to Go Around is often the wisest course of action.

20\. Ballooning. If you round-out too quickly or with too high an airspeed, the sudden increase in lift may cause the aircraft to climb before it touches the runway. If you do not recognize that you are high and continue to hold the landing attitude, there is a danger of stalling and dropping the aircraft onto the runway. This could result in structural damage to the landing gear. Normally the recovery action from ballooning is to go around. However, if the error is slight and you have sufficient airspeed, add power and complete another round-out for landing. In this case, set PCL to IDLE after the main wheels are on the ground.

21\. Bounce. If you should round-out too late, the aircraft may land and then bounce back into flight. In the event of a bounce, if the airspeed is sufficient that you are in no danger of stalling, hold the aircraft level until it starts to sink, round-out again, and then land. Remember that you can use power to assist the second round-out and to cushion the touchdown.

22\. Round-out Too High. If you should round-out too high and are in no danger of stalling, hold the aircraft level until it starts to sink, round-out again, and then land. Remember that you can use power to assist the second round-out and to cushion the touchdown.

23\. Crosswind Effect. Avoid skipping or drifting in a crosswind. The dihedral wing of the Harvard II is particularly sensitive to effects of a crosswind. You must ensure that you use the ailerons to prevent the aircraft from drifting sideways and possibly skipping (see Figure 4.5). Maintain crosswind inputs throughout the landing.

CAUTION

Never try to salvage a landing from a bad approach or from a poor land- ing. If you are in doubt as to the quality of your approach or landing, GO AROUND.

24\. Porpoising. Porpoising is a series of bounces from the main wheels to the nose wheel and back. It is usually caused by the nose-wheel contacting the ground before the main gear, or when the pilot over-controls during a correction for a bounce on land- ing. When the landing is attempted at a higher than normal speed, an abrupt backward or forward movement of the control column, at the point of touchdown, may induce porpoising. The combined reaction time of the pilot and control response is such that incorrect control movements may aggravate the porpoising and increase the magnitude of the bounce. This hazardous condition must be corrected immediately to prevent loss

Figure 4.5: Aircraft Skip on Runway

of control, structural damage, or both.

The corrective action is to select PCL to MAX and neutralize the controls. Complete a go around after the aircraft is safely airborne.

Fuel, weather, or other considerations may preclude the pilot from completing a go around. In such cases, the corrective action for a porpoise is to neutralize the controls until the aircraft starts to sink. Attempt to touch down on the main wheels by moving the control column progressively rearward. Do not pump the control column. Early recognition and recovery are essential.

4.12 CROSSWIND APPROACHES AND LANDINGS

25\. Drift becomes readily apparent during the landing transition. There are two com- monly used techniques to counter the effect of crosswind: the wing-down method and the combination method.

26\. Wing-Down Method. Lower the upwind wing sufficiently to eliminate drift, and simultaneously apply enough opposite rudder to keep the longitudinal axis in line with the centerline of the runway (see Figure 4.6). As the speed of the crosswind varies, so does the angle of bank required to keep the aircraft on the centerline of the runway. This is intentional cross-control. Because the wind may vary with altitude, continual small adjustments may be required to keep the aircraft lined up with the runway. During

Figure 4.7: Normal Crosswind Landing

the round-out and float period as the airspeed is decreasing, gradually increase the up- wind aileron and opposite rudder to keep straight until the aircraft lands on the upwind main wheel. Maintain the upwind aileron and sufficient rudder to keep straight as the downwind main wheel lowers to the runway (see Figure 4.7). When both wheels are firmly on the ground, maintain directional control with the same control inputs: rudder to maintain directional control and aileron into wind to maintain the centerline. As the aircraft slows, increase control deflection as required and then switch to brakes as the rudder becomes ineffective.

27\. Combination Method. Crab to eliminate drift on the final approach and transfer to the wing-down method during the round-out and float period. This method requires more finesse and better depth perception than the wing-down method, but is the pre- ferred method. Once established on final, crab into wind to compensate for drift. The wings should be level and the nose should be pointing towards the upwind side of the centerline. During the approach, you may have to make minor adjustments to compen- sate for variations in the wind. As you begin the round-out, smoothly apply rudder to line up the longitudinal axis of the aircraft with the centerline of the runway, and simul- taneously apply upwind aileron to lower the wing and prevent drift as you complete the

Figure 4.6: Correcting for Crosswind

landing.

28\. The following rules of good airmanship apply, particularly to crosswind landings:

a. Land on the runway centerline in order to leave room to correct for a swing in either direction and to allow for directional control errors during the ap- proach; and b. Never land while the aircraft is crabbing because severe stress is imposed on the landing gear. The wing-down method is to be utilized for all crosswind landings.

NOTE

Take-off flap or flapless landings should be considered during strong, gusty crosswind conditions.

4.13 TOUCH-AND-GO LANDINGS

29\. You will perform more touch-and-go than full-stop landings during basic training. Two types of touch-and-go landing techniques will be discussed. In the first type, the nose-wheel is lowered to the runway, and in the second type, the nose-wheel is held off the runway. In either case, crosswind technique is maintained throughout the touch-and- go.

30\. Lowering Nose-Wheel. Complete a normal landing. As the nose-wheel touches, smoothly advance the PCL to MAX. Be ready to compensate with rudder for the torque of the engine. Raise the nose to the take-off attitude which will vary slightly according to the amount of flap used, and allow the aircraft to fly off the runway. Do not re- trim the aircraft during the after-landing roll. Initially, the nose-up trim applied on final approach has little effect, but as the airspeed increases, you will have to re-trim. When the aircraft is well clear of the ground, raise the landing gear, and at 110 KIAS raise the flaps. Re-trim for the climb and complete the Post-Take-Off Check before attaining 147 KIAS.

31\. Nose-Wheel Held Off. When the main wheels touch down, smoothly advance the PCL to MAX. Be ready to compensate for the torque of the engine with rudder to maintain directional control. Hold the takeoff attitude by maintaining continuous back pressure, and allow the aircraft to fly off the ground. When the aircraft is well clear of the ground, raise the landing gear and, at 110 KIAS, raise the flaps. Re-trim for the climb and complete the Post-Take-Off Check prior to 147 KIAS.

4.14 THE TRAFFIC PATTERN

32\. The Harvard II traffic pattern is similar to that flown by high performance military aircraft. During your basic training, the traffic pattern is used to position/reposition

the aircraft at initial for the overhead break. The traffic pattern is normally flown at 1500 ft AGL or as otherwise defined by local operating procedures. In Moose Jaw the Harvard II traffic pattern is flown at 3000 ft MSL (approximately 1200 ft AGL) to de- conflict with Hawk traffic. The Harvard II pattern is flown at an airspeed of 220 KIAS (approximately 65% torque) which provides sufficient airspeed to glide the aircraft to the runway from most places in the pattern in the event of an engine failure. Carry out a circuit check on the downwind leg of every pattern. This check is to be completed abeam the mid-point of the runway in use.

33\. Use 60 degree bank turns throughout the pattern, except for the last turn to initial, when the bank should range from 45–60 degrees, as required to line up on the extended runway centreline. The 45 degree heading change on the entry leg is designed to make the turn to initial easier to judge. Maintain a vigilant lookout throughout the traffic pattern, but even more so prior to the turns. Traffic normally joins the traffic pattern via one of the extended legs; therefore, the greatest potential for conflict exists in the corners of the pattern.

4.15 JOINING THE TRAFFIC PATTERN

34\. The primary method of joining the traffic pattern is via one of the extended legs of the published traffic pattern (See Figure 4.8). Entry to the traffic pattern shall be made in such a manner as to avoid “cutting off” other aircraft, conforming as closely as possible to the published pattern.

35\. When joining the traffic pattern, ensure that the aircraft is established on the ex- tended rejoin track, at pattern altitude and airspeed at least 1 NM from the published traffic pattern to ensure effective lookout prior to entry. Remember that aircraft already established in the traffic pattern normally have the right of way. If any conflict exists during the rejoin, take positive action to avoid the traffic and reposition the aircraft for another rejoin attempt.

4.16 STRAIGHT-IN APPROACHES FROM THE TRAFFIC PATTERN

36\. As was previously mentioned, a portion of the traffic pattern will be used to repo- sition the aircraft for a straight-in approach if desired. Fly the normal traffic pattern ground track and speeds until abeam the approach end of the runway in use. Reduce the PCL to approximately 20% torque and commence a descent at 220 KIAS to 300’ below normal traffic pattern altitude. Once level, leave the PCL at the reduced torque setting, allowing the aircraft to decelerate. Continue on the downwind leg until approximately 1 NM beyond the base leg of the OHB traffic pattern. Commence an energy depleting 45 degree angle of bank turn, and roll out on base leg. This will minimize any delays configuring the aircraft as it should roll out on the base leg below the 147 KIAS max gear extension speed. Once established on base leg for the straight-in, continue with the procedures described in Section 4.6.

37\. Accurate altitude control and lookout is critical while approaching the base leg turn point. Considering the altitudes flown in Moose Jaw, other Harvard II traffic may be joining the traffic pattern with just 300 ft of altitude separation above you.

4.17 THE OVERHEAD BREAK

38\. The overhead break begins at initial. Transmit your position and intentions to the tower (i.e. APOLLO 4, INITIAL LEFT/RIGHT STOP). Apply crab as required to track along the extended runway centreline and maintain traffic pattern altitude. See Figure 4.10. Moreover, to maintain an effective lookout, scanning up and to the left/right will provide situational awareness of possible PFL traffic.

39\. The Break. The break is a 180-degree level turn used to place the aircraft down- wind and reduce the airspeed in preparation to initiate the pre-landing check (See Figure 4.10). At a point approximately 2000 ft past the runway threshold, assuming average wind conditions (10 kts headwind component), smoothly roll into a 60-degree bank turn in the appropriate direction, reduce PCL to IDLE and trim the aircraft as necessary. During the final portion of the turn, or while rolling out, advance the PCL slightly to get the engine out of the idle setting. This will minimize the engine spool up time when the power is later needed. Roll out on downwind, correcting for known wind conditions if required. Ideally, the roll out will position the aircraft parallel to the runway prior to reaching the point abeam the runway threshold.

40\. Downwind. Maintain traffic pattern altitude, carry out the pre-landing check and select flaps to T/O. Power up to arrest the rate at which the airspeed is decreasing (ap- proximately 35% torque). Remember to constantly re-trim as the airspeed and configu-
